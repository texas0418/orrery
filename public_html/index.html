Orrery

A mechanical model of the solar system, with no mechanism.

What you're looking at

The positions on screen are not an animation loop or a decorative approximation. Every frame, the page solves Kepler's equation for each planet from the orbital elements JPL publishes for the interval 1800–2050, and draws the answer. Set the date to any moment in that range and the picture is where those worlds genuinely were, or genuinely will be, to within a few arcminutes.

The camera starts above the plane of Earth's orbit, on its north side, where everything runs counter-clockwise. You can move it. The tick ring around the outside is ecliptic longitude, zero at the vernal equinox, marked every 30° with the sign that shares that stretch of sky.

What's honest and what isn't

  • Positions: real. Elliptical, correctly oriented, correctly phased.
  • Orbit shapes: computed from the same elements as the planets on them, then warped by whatever the Spread slider is set to. Push Spread to 100 and you are looking at the true ellipses, with the Sun at a focus rather than the centre. Select a body and the two ends of its long axis get marked, which is where that stops being a technicality — Mercury's distance from the Sun swings by a third over its 88-day year.
  • Distances: compressed by default. The Spread slider is an exponent: at 100 the layout is linearly true to scale, at which point Mercury sits a pixel from the Sun and is effectively invisible. At the default 45 you can see the whole system at once. The numbers in the panels are always the true ones.
  • Body sizes: not to scale, and could never be. At this zoom Earth would be far smaller than one pixel. The one place the page does enlarge things, the Approach view, prints the factor it is using.
  • Inclination: real, and now visible. The orrery is drawn with a camera in three dimensions, so the few degrees each orbit is tilted out of the plane of Earth's orbit are on screen rather than projected away. Press Edge on and select Pluto: 17° of tilt, swinging clear of the thin sheet everything else keeps to. Each planet drops a dotted line to that plane so you can read its height off directly.
  • Compression does not distort direction. Spread scales how far a body sits from the Sun but never which way, so the inclination and the ecliptic longitude on screen are exact at every setting. Only distance is negotiable.

The asteroid belt

The gap between Mars and Jupiter is not empty, so four of its residents are drawn in it: Ceres, Pallas, Juno and Vesta — the first four found, between 1801 and 1807, and all four were counted as planets for about fifty years before anyone realised how many more there were.

They are worth having for the same reason Pluto is. Pallas is tilted almost 35° out of the plane, steeper than Pluto and steeper than anything else of its size; press Edge on and it climbs clear of everything. Juno and Pallas are both markedly eccentric. The belt is not a tidy ring.

Their elements are handled differently from the planets' and it is worth saying how. JPL publishes a current osculating orbit for each, which is exact today and drifts badly once you leave — Ceres alone ends up 14° out of place by 1800, because Jupiter has been pulling on it the whole way. So instead I sampled each orbit from JPL Horizons every five years across 1800–2050 and fitted secular rates to the result, which is the same treatment the planets get. That brings the worst error anywhere in the window down to 0.7°. A fifth, Hygiea, would not fit that cleanly — it was still nearly 5° out — so it is not here. Being absent seemed better than being wrong.

They are left out of the sky view and the five-year sweep, since only Vesta ever gets near naked-eye brightness and none of them belongs in a list of things to go outside and look at.

Events

Select a planet and its card searches forward from the displayed date for the next time it matters. For the outer planets that's opposition — Earth passing directly between it and the Sun, when the planet rises at sunset and is closest and brightest all year. For Mercury and Venus it's greatest elongation, the furthest they ever stray from the Sun's glare, which is the only reason you ever get to see them.

The Coming up tab widens that to the whole system: one pass computes every body's direction as seen from Earth for each of the next 1,826 days, and the oppositions, elongations and close pairings all fall out of the same table. Candidates are then refined by bisection down to the minute. Click any entry and the model flies to that moment. A conjunction is only two worlds falling along the same line of sight from where we happen to be standing, and seeing them from above makes plain how little they have to do with each other — one is often four times further away than the other. Push Spread to 100 while you're there and the alignment becomes literal, because true scale is the only setting where angles on screen are angles in the sky; the compression bends every sight line that doesn't pass through the Sun.

Pairings are searched among Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn, listed when they close to within 3°, and dropped when both sit inside 15° of the Sun — technically a conjunction, but lost in the glare and no use to anyone. Apparent brightness isn't computed, and neither is the Moon.

Earth's-eye

The Sky switch keeps the same positions and asks a different question: not where the planets are, but where you would have to look. The heliocentric vectors get rotated into equatorial coordinates, precessed from J2000 to the date on the clock, and turned into altitude and azimuth for wherever you're standing. Sunrise and sunset come out within a minute of published tables, which is the check I used to know the chain was right.

Your location is guessed from your browser's time zone, which is a city-sized guess and needs no permission. Click the place name at the top of the Tonight panel to stand somewhere else — search a hundred and forty cities, jump to the poles or the equator, or type coordinates for anywhere on Earth. You can also hand over your real position; that one asks the browser, and the answer never leaves this page. There is nowhere for it to go: no server, no request, no storage.

The far latitudes are worth the trip. Put yourself in Svalbard in June and the Sun circles overhead without setting all day; stand at the South Pole in the same week and it sits at −23° below the horizon and stays there, because at the poles a body's altitude is simply its declination and nothing rises or sets at all.

The stars are real, 1,637 of them down to magnitude 5, roughly what a good dark sky gives you. The Moon comes from an abridged lunar theory that is good to a few arcminutes — enough to put it in the right place among the stars and to draw its phase, not enough to predict an occultation.

The loop

This is the thing I built the mode for. Select a planet, leave the trail on, and you get its own position plotted for every few days over the months either side — held against the stars, because that's the frame it's moving through.

Mars will draw you a loop. For most of the time it slides east against the stars; then for a couple of months it stops, reverses, backs westward, stops again and resumes. Nothing about Mars changes. Earth, on a shorter, faster orbit, is overtaking it on the inside, and the loop is what our own passing looks like from here. This is the appearance that cost astronomy fifteen hundred years of epicycles, and you can watch it resolve into an overtaking manoeuvre by flipping to the Orrery view on the same date.

Approach

Select a planet and press Approach. The camera flies in and the marker becomes a world, mapped with real spacecraft imagery.

What is computed rather than drawn: the orientation, from the IAU's published pole direction and prime-meridian rate for each body, so the face turned towards you is the face that was turned that way on the date on the clock; and the lighting, from the true Sun direction, so the terminator falls where it falls and the panel tells you the latitude and longitude the Sun is standing over. Set Earth to noon UT and Africa is centred under the Sun. Advance twelve hours and the Pacific is.

The sizes are a lie, and the page says by how much. At any honest scale you never see a globe — Earth is far under a pixel from anywhere you would want to watch the solar system from. So the approach view enlarges, and prints the factor it is using: ×3.8 thousand actual size, recalculated for your exact camera. Nothing else on the page is exaggerated, and this is the one place that needed saying out loud.

Other things I have not pretended about. The bodies are drawn as spheres; Saturn is really flattened by a tenth and Jupiter by a sixteenth. Saturn's rings come from measured radii — the C, B and A rings with the Cassini division between the last two — but neither casts a shadow on the other, and edge-on they nearly vanish, which is true and is why they look so thin in 2026, a year after the plane crossing. Venus is its cloud deck, because that is all anyone has ever seen from outside. Uranus and Neptune are very nearly featureless in reality, so any banding on them is interpretation, not observation. The Moon's orientation omits the small libration terms and is good to a degree or two.

The maps are fetched only when you actually go and look, one body at a time, from this same domain. The page still weighs about sixty kilobytes until you ask for a world.

You can keep going in until you are in low orbit. The globe is ray-traced against a real sphere rather than shaded as a flat disc, so as you close in the limb curves away and you see less than a hemisphere, exactly as you would from a few hundred kilometres up. The panel reports the implied altitude and how many radii out you are.

Pluto and the four asteroids can be visited too, but there are no maps for them here — so they arrive as shape and sunlight only. The lit fraction is real and the size factor is real; the face is not, and the panel says so rather than inventing a surface.

In Sky, the Moon is the exception that needs no exaggeration at all: it subtends half a degree, so narrow the field below about twelve degrees and it is simply drawn at the size it is, phase and all.

Twenty moons

Go to a planet and it arrives as a world. Scroll out from there and its moons come with it, drawn to the same exaggeration as the planet, so the system is internally to scale even though the whole thing is enlarged. Jupiter with the Galileans wheeling round it is the view Galileo had in 1610, and the one that ended the geocentric universe — but you have to pull back to see it, because no one distance can hold both. Our Moon orbits at 59.5 Earth-radii and Io at 6 of Jupiter's: the altitude that frames either system leaves the planet at its centre a few pixels across, and the altitude that shows you the planet leaves the moons off the screen. That is the geometry, not a setting.

Click one, or pick it from the list beneath its planet, and you can go to it the same way you go to a planet — the camera flies over and stands off it as a world, with low orbit a scroll further in. Its planet and its sibling moons come with it, placed by a perspective camera at the true altitude and sized by true angular radius. Where the planet appears is a question of which way you are facing rather than how far out you are — it lies within atan(distance ÷ altitude) of the moon, so from close in it is usually behind you, and pulling back gathers it towards the moon until it cannot be anywhere else. Stand off Europa and Jupiter is 11.9° wide — twenty-three full Moons — filling a quarter of the view. That is not a flourish; it is what the geometry gives when you stop compressing it.

Fourteen of them are mapped, from spacecraft: Voyager and Galileo for Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto; Cassini for Titan, Enceladus, Tethys, Dione, Rhea and Iapetus; Voyager 2 for Triton; New Horizons for Charon; Viking for Phobos; and our own Moon. Their orientation needs no rotation table, because a synchronous satellite keeps one face to its planet — which is also the IAU convention for these bodies, longitude zero pointing at the primary. So the hemisphere turned towards you is the one that really is, and it is the same hemisphere anyone has ever charted.

Several of those maps are monochrome, because the controlled mosaics they come from are. Io is famously sulphur-yellow and arrives here in grey. I would rather hand you the real basemap without its colour than tint it to look like the photograph you were expecting. Iapetus loses nothing at all: its dark and bright hemispheres are a brightness difference, and that is exactly what a monochrome mosaic records.

Coverage is honest too. Triton was photographed by one spacecraft on one pass, so its map covers the hemisphere Voyager 2 saw and no more. Five moons have no map, for a reason worth knowing. Voyager 2 passed Uranus once, in 1986, with the planet's pole aimed almost at the Sun — so only the southern hemispheres of Miranda, Ariel, Umbriel, Titania and Oberon have ever been photographed at all. There is no global mosaic to have. Those five and Deimos arrive as shape and sunlight only: the size factor and the lit fraction are real, the face is not.

They are in the sky as well. Narrow the field below about a degree and Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto string out along the ecliptic beside Jupiter, arranged as they actually are that night — which is a real observing aid, because they are a binocular target and the pattern changes every few hours.

Getting them right took two goes. JPL publishes mean elements for satellites, referenced to each planet's Laplace plane, and they looked ideal. They are not: their orbital phases are out by as much as 178°, which would have put Io on the wrong side of Jupiter — the most convincing kind of wrong. So instead each moon was sampled from JPL Horizons every 150 to 731 days across 1800–2050 and its elements fitted to that, exactly as the asteroids were. A moon keeps to its planet's equatorial plane and a planet's pole barely moves, so the inclination and node in ecliptic coordinates stay nearly constant and a straight line through them holds.

Every fitted one is checked against Horizons at four dates spread across the whole window — nineteen of the twenty, our own Moon being the exception, since it comes from a lunar theory rather than a fit. Fourteen of the nineteen land within a degree and twelve within a third of one; Charon, Titan, Dione and Io are inside a twentieth. The stubborn ones are Triton at 4.9° and Miranda at 4.6°, then Tethys, Phobos and Deimos between 1.8 and 2.3.

Phobos was the interesting one. It went round Mars three times a day and a straight line in longitude put it 12° out — because Phobos is spiralling in. Tides are dragging it down about two centimetres a year and its orbital period is measurably shortening, so its mean motion is accelerating and no constant rate can describe it. Giving the longitude a quadratic term brought it to 2.2°. That is a real physical effect showing up as a curve in a fit, which is the sort of thing that makes a number worth chasing.

Mimas is missing. It is locked in a two-to-one resonance with Tethys and librates, and no fixed ellipse can follow that: the fit put it 111° along its orbit from where it belongs, so it is not here at all. Same reasoning as Hygiea in the belt.

Controls

  • In the Orrery, drag to swing the camera around the system, scroll or pinch to move in and out, shift-drag to slide the whole view. Once you are at a body that same scroll becomes your altitude above it — down to low orbit one way, out past its moons and back to the solar system the other. Top, Tilt and Edge on are the three angles worth having; Fly to takes the camera to whatever is selected and Approach goes closer still.
  • Click a body to select it. That also marks the ends of its long axis — perihelion and aphelion — which is where the off-centre Sun stops being an abstraction.
  • Space play/pause · ←/→ step a day · N return to now · Esc deselect
  • The playback buttons step through speeds from a minute per second up to a decade per second, in both directions. In Sky they stop at six hours per second and say so: past that the sky turns further between one frame and the next than a frame can show, so it stops being motion and becomes noise. The day/night tint is also rate-limited in real time, so scrubbing across a century fades rather than strobes.
  • In Sky, drag to look around, scroll to change the field of view, and click a body to walk the view over to it.
  • The address bar keeps the date, selection, view and location, so anything you land on is a link you can send to someone.

If you want more than this

This page is small on purpose, and most of these outclass it. If it made you curious about something it can't answer, go here instead — I'd rather point you at the better tool than pretend to be it.

  • Stellarium — the reference planetarium. Free, open source, hundreds of thousands of stars, deep-sky objects, telescope control. There's a browser version at stellarium-web.org if you don't want to install anything.
  • Celestia — the one that does what people usually mean by flying through the solar system: leave the planets behind and keep going, out to other stars.
  • NASA's Eyes on the Solar System — has the thing I can't. Real spacecraft, real mission trajectories, live positions of actual probes, straight from JPL.
  • In-The-Sky.org — Dominic Ford's site, and far more thorough than my Coming up tab: conjunctions, occultations, eclipses, meteor showers, all with proper charts.
  • TheSkyLive — live positions for the planets, and the best of these for comets: finder charts, brightness curves, and something to look at long before a comet is news.
  • Heavens-Above — the one for satellites. When the space station goes over your house, this will tell you.
  • JPL Horizons — not a pretty site. It's the authoritative ephemeris service that everything else, this page included, is ultimately checked against. If you ever want to know whether I've told you the truth, that's where you'd go and find out.
  • 100,000 Stars — the solar neighbourhood in three dimensions, and lovely for its own sake.
  • WorldWide Telescope — real survey imagery of the actual sky rather than a simulation of it.
  • Solar System Scope — a polished 3D model, and the source of the surface maps used here.

If you can't see the screen

The whole visual is a canvas, which is to say it is a picture with no text in it. So the canvas carries a written description of what it currently shows — which bodies are above your horizon and where, or where every planet is on the map — and that description is kept current in a polite live region as you scrub, fly or switch views. The panels beside it were already real text and already read properly.

If your system asks for reduced motion, the camera arrives instead of gliding and the page stops animating. Keyboard focus is visible everywhere, Escape closes whatever is open, and the dimmest text on the page was lifted until it passed contrast — it had been sitting at 3:1, which is not enough, and is now near 5:1.

Colophon

One HTML file and nine surface maps. No frameworks, no libraries, no build step, no server, no analytics, no cookies, nothing phoning home — the maps come from this domain, on demand, and only if you go looking for one. Two webfonts, a packed star catalogue, and about sixteen hundred lines of hand-written canvas and astronomy. It will keep working, unchanged, for as long as browsers run JavaScript.
