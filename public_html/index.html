Orrery

A mechanical model of the solar system, with no mechanism.

What you're looking at

The positions on screen are not an animation loop or a decorative approximation. Every frame, the page solves Kepler's equation for each planet from the orbital elements JPL publishes for the interval 1800–2050, and draws the answer. Set the date to any moment in that range and the picture is where those worlds genuinely were, or genuinely will be, to within a few arcminutes.

You're looking down on the plane of Earth's orbit from its north side, so everything runs counter-clockwise. The tick ring around the outside is ecliptic longitude, zero at the vernal equinox, marked every 30° with the sign that shares that stretch of sky.

What's honest and what isn't

  • Positions: real. Elliptical, correctly oriented, correctly phased.
  • Orbit shapes: real ellipses, drawn from the same elements — the Sun sits at a focus, not the centre. Mercury and Pluto are visibly off-centre, as they should be.
  • Distances: compressed by default. The Spread slider is an exponent: at 100 the layout is linearly true to scale, at which point Mercury sits a pixel from the Sun and is effectively invisible. At the default 45 you can see the whole system at once. The numbers in the panels are always the true ones.
  • Body sizes: not to scale, and could never be. At this zoom Earth would be far smaller than one pixel.
  • Flattened: orbits are tilted a few degrees out of the ecliptic; this view projects that away. Pluto's 17° tilt is the one you'd really notice.

Events

Select a planet and the card searches forward from the displayed date for the next time it matters. For the outer planets that's opposition — Earth passing directly between it and the Sun, when the planet rises at sunset and is closest and brightest all year. For Mercury and Venus it's greatest elongation, the furthest they ever stray from the Sun's glare, which is the only reason you ever get to see them. The search steps a day at a time and then bisects down to the minute.

Controls

  • Drag to pan, scroll or pinch to zoom, click a body to select it.
  • Space play/pause · ←/→ step a day · N return to now · Esc deselect
  • The playback buttons step through speeds from an hour per second up to a decade per second, in both directions.

Colophon

One HTML file. No frameworks, no libraries, no build step, no server, no analytics, no cookies, nothing phoning home. Two webfonts and about nine hundred lines of hand-written canvas and orbital mechanics. It will keep working, unchanged, for as long as browsers run JavaScript.

Elements from Approximate Positions of the Major Planets (E. M. Standish, JPL Solar System Dynamics), the low-precision table valid 1800–2050. The timeline stops at both ends of that window rather than extrapolate into fiction.
